[ NSERC CPRA – Dunlap Fellow ] · Dunlap Institute for Astronomy and Astrophysics · University of Toronto

Tri V. Nguyen

I am a near-field cosmologist working at the interface of astrophysics, statistics and machine learning. I am interested in the particle nature of dark matter and how it shapes structure formation on the smallest scales, using the dwarf galaxies and stellar streams of the Milky Way as my laboratory. Some of the questions I work on:

  • Are the centers of dwarf galaxies cusped or cored, and what does that tell us about dark matter?
  • How many dark subhalos orbit the Milky Way, and can stellar streams detect them?
  • What are the shape, mass profile, and assembly history of the Milky Way’s dark matter halo?

On the statistics and machine learning side, I am interested in developing novel and robust inference techniques, and I work on making them reliable, e.g. calibrated posteriors, robustness to the mismatch between simulations and data, and principled model comparison.

Before Toronto I was a CIERA Postdoctoral Fellow at Northwestern University and a postdoctoral affiliate of the NSF–Simons SkAI Institute. I completed my Ph.D. in Physics at MIT in 2024 with Prof. Lina Necib, where I was also a member of the NSF AI Institute for Artificial Intelligence and Fundamental Interactions (IAIFI). I received my B.S. in Physics & Astronomy from the University of Rochester in 2019.

// Research

Dwarf galaxies

Dwarf galaxies are the most dark-matter-dominated systems in the Universe, and how their dark matter is distributed is a direct test of dark matter physics. I am interested in developing novel, robust inference techniques that extract the dark matter content of a dwarf from the kinematics of a few dozen stars. I am also interested in tackling the systematics of spectroscopic surveys, from selection functions to membership selection, and actively work with observers to fold them into the inference and to apply it to new observations of the Milky Way's dwarfs.

Stellar streams

Stellar streams are the tidal remnants of dwarf galaxies and globular clusters. As stellar streams orbit the Milky Way, they are perturbed by dark matter subhalos, which create visible features such as gaps and spurs in the stream that can be traced back to the subhalo. I am actively working on several projects that use stellar kinematics from large photometric and spectroscopic surveys to hunt for subhalos, using machine learning and simulation-based inference. I am especially interested in the model-selection side of the problem: whether a stream's features can be explained without a subhalo at all, and how to answer that question with a statistical framework that is robust to the choices made along the way.

Halo assembly

How dark matter halos grow through mergers shapes the properties of the galaxies they host. Fast, accurate models of assembly histories and full merger trees are challenging to build. Analytic methods miss the dependence on environment and internal structure, and N-body simulations are expensive. With the Galaxy Formation group at the CCA, I developed generative models that produce assembly histories and merger trees with the statistical fidelity of simulations at a fraction of the cost, as a backbone for semi-analytic galaxy models.

Galaxy-halo connection

The connection between galaxies and their dark matter halos is central to cosmology and to testing dark matter models. Hydrodynamic simulations are among the leading methods to study the galaxy-halo connection, but their computational cost limits their ability to fully explore the dark matter and baryonic parameter space. I authored NeHOD, a variational diffusion model that paints galaxies and subhalos onto dark matter halos, which the DREAMS collaboration uses to turn a thousand simulations into predictions across the whole parameter space.
